# 2026 Word Survey results (n = 901)

Every year we run a survey to check which Toki Pona words people report as being part of their active vocabulary, which helps us update resources to be better aligned with how speakers see their own language. It's that time of the year again to review this year's results! 

## Number of respondents

2024, 2025, 2026 have fallen in the range of 900-1000 respondents, though note that about half of the respondents each time are *new* - that is, they haven't taken a survey the previous year. There are two main reasons for it:
1. There is *activity/survey fatigue*: the surveys have been concentrated around big community events, with lots to do, and only so much a less-engaged person can reasonably pay attention to. This year we've offered 4 forms to fill out (the word survey, the glyph survey, the demographic survey, suno pi toki pona attendance / feedback), so it's natural for people to only tackle a few of these and move on to other activities. We are discussing spreading out surveying across the year to mitigate this.
2. The Toki Pona community has in the past several years reached a fairly steady inflow and outflow of participants. So while the number of people capable of speaking Toki Pona keeps going up, activity on platforms that we can observe (and ping) does not increase or decrease drastically.

## Methodology

This year we've changed our **core word** polling. By 2024 we've noticed a pattern of an unnatural gap between core and common words:

| word    | score (2024) |
|---------|--------------|
| esun    | 99           |
| monsi   | 99           |
| mu      | 98           |
| unpa    | 98           |
| pu      | 95           |
| ale     | 93           |
| monsuta | 84           |
| kin     | 83           |
| tonsi   | 82           |
| meli    | 82           |
| mije    | 82           |

At its root was the **faulty assumption that we can assume "yes" as a default answer for core words**. This was especially striking among people who only recently started learning the language: their answers would average around 60-70% for common words, not because they are deliberately choosing not to use them, but because they simply haven't solidified their knowledge of the vocabulary. This lack of certainty **also affected low-frequency core words** such as *selo* or *monsi*, but the responder had the option to skip them with a decisive answer, and most chose to do that out of convenience, creating this discontinuity.

We've identified this problem back in 2025, but had no time to address it. This year, we have! We have done away with "skippable" core words that assume a particular answer. But polling 117 entries is likewise difficult: the responder will experience fatigue and start answering without paying much attention, which skews the results towards uniformity. Instead we chose to show a responder **just a sample** of core words, which they can fully focus on, with the understanding that we have enough responders in total for each sample to become a statistically meaningful approximation of the community as a whole. A lower sample size amplifies noise in the data, but in exchange we get to have a fully interpretable dataset without a discontinuity.

By popular demand we've also given people an option to fill out every core word *if they really want to*, which is an option ~3/8ths of the participants took. I had concerns for how this affects interpretability, but I've compared the two subsets of data side by side and they differ very little. The final dataset simply accepts both data sources.

## Observations

### Core-common boundary

The methodological change was very successful! The core-common discontinuity has been eliminated. The core words, which previously were distributed in the 100-95% range, have now spread out across 100-87%, intersecting with the highest-ranked common words:

| word    | score (2026) |
|---------|--------------|
| esun    | 92           |
| lupa    | 91           |
| nena    | 91           |
| monsi   | 90           |
| sinpin  | 90           |
| mu      | 89           |
| selo    | 89           |
| kin     | 87           |
| unpa    | 87           |
| monsuta | 86           |
| meli    | 79           |
| mije    | 79           |
| tonsi   | 77           |

Since "core words" as a conceptual category still exists (all words that are used by essentially everyone who has learned the language, and are more or less indispensable for the concepts they describe), we've agreed to move the core/common boundary to 85% to match how the curve has shifted now that we have better core word data.

This makes 119 core words, including **kin** and **monsuta**.

### Lang's book -u word series

| word | score (2025) | score (2026) | Δ   |
|------|--------------|--------------|-----|
| pu   | 94           | 56           | -38 |
| ku   | 61           | 45           | -16 |
| su   | 48           | 30           | -18 |

The Linku dictionary currently documents 3 words coined by Sonja Lang - those being **pu**, **ku**, and **su** - that have to do with works published by her. Responses to these three words have shifted significantly, putting all three in the "uncommon" grouping. The 38-point shift for **pu** is unprecedentedly large in the past few years of polling. I see two main reasons for this change:
1. Two of Sonja Lang's most recent publications in late 2025 - early 2026 have brought new interest to the -u series words. Frequent topics of discussion included what these words really mean, the relationship between authorial intent and de facto use, retconning, and whether they are to be considered proper names or not. It is likely that these discussions genuinely affected many people's view of these words, and may have popularised previously lesser seen nasins, such as referring to Lang's books as a typical, capitalised proper name (e.g. *lipu Pu*, *lipu Ku*).
2. The shift seen for the **pu** entry is compounded - it includes both the shift in the community nasin, and the methodological core word change. In other words, people who may have wanted to score **pu** lower in 2025 may have forgotten to do so due to being able to skip it, which has been streamlined now. It is impossible to know for certain where **pu** would have landed in 2025 had we implemented the change when we wanted to, but I estimate somewhere in the 80-70% range - together with most common words, and slightly above **ku**.
